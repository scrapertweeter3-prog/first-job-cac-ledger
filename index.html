The first job is a customer-acquisition cost

The oldest trick in freelancing is the loss-leader first job: bid low, win the review, raise rates after. Priced honestly it is a marketing spend in labor. Take a $500 first contract at $25 an hour when the going rate is $40. The money is the same, but the work runs 20 hours instead of 12.5, and the missing 7.5 hours at your own rate is $300. That $300 is the customer-acquisition cost, paid in hours instead of ad dollars.

Whether the trade was smart depends entirely on the re-booking. A one-and-done client leaves you with $500 minus $300 of discount, which is $200 for 20 hours, a $10 hourly rate. A client who re-books four more times at full rate hands you $3,200 on top, and the same $300 discount becomes 8 percent of first-year revenue, cheaper than most ways to buy a customer.

The blended rate shows the discount burning off. One job: $500 over 20 hours, $10 an hour. Two jobs: $1,300 over 40, $32.50. Three: $2,100 over 60, $35. Five: $3,700 over 100, $37. Each repeat booking dilutes the giveaway, from 23 percent of revenue after two jobs to 14 after three to 8 after five, which is why the discount only makes sense on work that re-books: monthly content, ongoing maintenance, retainer blocks. On one-shot projects like a logo or a single audit, the $300 never gets diluted and the deal stays at $10 an hour forever.

Job size changes the bill faster than most bidders expect. The 15-dollar gap is per hour of the discounted job, so a 10-hour first contract costs $150 to win and a 30-hour one costs $450. A "small" rate cut on a big first job is the most expensive acquisition channel on the platform. Bid the discount by the re-booking odds, never by the review count.
